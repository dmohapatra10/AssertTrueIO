Flaky Test Scoring

Prerequisites: Recommended Fixes

What is this?

A real test suite with dozens or hundreds of tests will genuinely have more than one flaky test at a time. This final page is about prioritization — not every flaky test deserves the same urgency, and a genuine score makes that explicit rather than leaving it to whoever complains loudest.

A Genuine Scoring Formula — Not Just Fail Rate

def score_flaky_test(candidate, test_metadata):
    fail_rate = candidate["fail_rate"]

    # IMPORTANCE — does this test cover something genuinely
    # critical? Checkout matters more than a cosmetic UI detail.
    importance = test_metadata.get("criticality", 1)  # 1-3 scale

    # FREQUENCY — how often does this test actually RUN? A
    # flaky test in a suite that runs 50x/day disrupts far
    # more than one running once a week.
    runs_per_day = test_metadata.get("runs_per_day", 1)

    # Combined score — deliberately weighted toward IMPACT,
    # not just raw fail rate alone
    score = fail_rate * importance * min(runs_per_day, 20)
    return round(score, 2)

Why Fail Rate Alone Genuinely Isn't Enough to Prioritize By

Test A: "cosmetic tooltip alignment check"
  fail_rate = 0.4 (genuinely quite flaky)
  importance = 1 (low — cosmetic, not business-critical)
  runs_per_day = 2 (runs rarely)
  score = 0.4 × 1 × 2 = 0.8

Test B: "cart total updates after adding an item" (this
  chapter's own running example)
  fail_rate = 0.2 (LESS flaky than Test A, numerically)
  importance = 3 (HIGH — directly affects checkout, real revenue)
  runs_per_day = 20 (runs on every commit, genuinely frequent)
  score = 0.2 × 3 × 20 = 12.0

Despite a LOWER raw fail rate, Test B scores FIFTEEN TIMES
higher — genuinely reflecting that its real-world IMPACT
(business criticality × how often it disrupts the team) is
far greater, even though it fails less often per run.

🧠 Think About It

Why might sorting purely by fail rate — fixing the "most flaky" test first, regardless of anything else — lead a team to consistently work on the wrong things? Fail rate alone measures only how OFTEN a test is inconsistent — it says nothing about how much that inconsistency actually MATTERS. A rarely-run, low-importance test with a high fail rate genuinely disrupts a team far less than a frequently-run, business-critical test with a moderate fail rate, even though the raw statistic alone would rank them in the opposite order. Prioritizing by IMPACT, not just RATE, is what actually matches limited fixing time to where it genuinely helps most.

⚠️ Common Mistake

Prioritizing flaky test fixes purely by whichever failure is most visible or recently annoying, rather than by a genuine, consistent scoring approach. This page's own example shows a numerically LESS flaky test can genuinely deserve MORE urgent attention once real business impact and disruption frequency are properly accounted for.

💡 Quick Tip

The min(runs_per_day, 20) cap in this page's formula is a deliberate choice — beyond some point, additional daily runs stop meaningfully changing the PRIORITIZATION decision (a test running 200 times a day isn't genuinely 10x more urgent than one running 20 times), and an uncapped multiplier could let an extreme value dominate the score in a way that doesn't reflect genuine, proportional priority.

Best Practices

  • Score flaky tests by genuine impact — combining fail rate, business importance, and disruption frequency — not fail rate alone.
  • Recognize that a numerically less-flaky test can genuinely deserve higher priority once real impact is properly weighted in.
  • Cap multipliers where appropriate to avoid an extreme single value dominating a prioritization score disproportionately.

🎯 Key Takeaway

  • Fail rate alone is a poor, incomplete basis for prioritizing which flaky tests to fix first — real impact requires weighing importance and frequency too.
  • A genuine scoring formula can reveal that a less numerically flaky test deserves more urgent attention than a more flaky but lower-impact one.
  • This closes the chapter's full arc — from recognizing a flaky test exists, through understanding and diagnosing why, to genuinely fixing it, to deciding which ones are actually worth fixing first with real, limited time.

Why does this page's formula cap runs_per_day at 20 rather than using the raw, uncapped value?▾

Without a cap, a test running an extremely high number of times daily could dominate the score through sheer volume alone, in a way that doesn't proportionally reflect genuinely greater priority beyond a reasonable point.

What an UNCAPPED multiplier would risk:
A test running 500 times a day (perhaps part of a very
  high-frequency smoke-test suite) with even a LOW fail rate
  and LOW importance could still produce an ENORMOUS score
  purely from the runs_per_day multiplier — potentially
  outranking a genuinely more IMPORTANT, more frequently
  IMPACTFUL test just because of raw run VOLUME.

Why the CAP genuinely helps:
min(runs_per_day, 20) ensures that beyond a reasonable
  threshold, ADDITIONAL daily runs stop disproportionately
  inflating the score — the DIFFERENCE between running 20
  times a day and 500 times a day doesn't genuinely represent
  a PROPORTIONAL difference in how urgently a fix is needed,
  which the cap correctly reflects.

This is a genuinely practical design choice worth recognizing — a scoring formula's individual factors sometimes need deliberate bounds to keep the OVERALL score meaningful and proportional, rather than letting one extreme input value dominate the result in a way that doesn't reflect genuine priority.

Explain why "importance" is described using a 1-3 scale rather than a more granular, continuous value in this page's formula.▾

Business criticality is genuinely a coarser, more judgment-based assessment than something like fail rate — forcing it into an artificially precise continuous scale would suggest a false level of precision that the underlying judgment doesn't actually support.

Why a COARSE scale is genuinely more honest here:
"Is this test's feature CRITICAL, IMPORTANT, or MINOR to the
  business" is a genuine, real JUDGMENT CALL — reasonably
  categorized into a FEW clear tiers (checkout = critical,
  a secondary feature = important, cosmetic detail = minor),
  but not something meaningfully more precise than that in
  most real, practical situations.

Why a CONTINUOUS scale would be GENUINELY misleading:
Assigning "importance = 2.347" would suggest a PRECISION of
  judgment that doesn't actually exist — business criticality
  isn't measured with that kind of fine-grained accuracy in
  practice, and pretending otherwise would be FALSE precision,
  not genuine rigor.

This connects to a broader, important principle: a genuinely well-designed metric should reflect the actual precision of its underlying judgment — fail rate can genuinely be precise (it's a real count), while importance is genuinely more of a coarse, human judgment call, and the formula's design appropriately reflects that real difference.

Why might "fix cost" (mentioned in this page's title but not explicitly in the formula) be worth adding as a genuine factor, beyond fail rate, importance, and frequency?▾

This is a genuinely fair observation — the page's title mentions fix cost, but the actual formula only captures impact, not effort; a complete prioritization should genuinely weigh both, since a high-impact but very expensive-to-fix test might reasonably be deprioritized relative to an almost-as-impactful but genuinely quick fix.

A more COMPLETE formula, incorporating fix cost:
def score_flaky_test_v2(candidate, test_metadata):
    impact_score = score_flaky_test(candidate, test_metadata)  # as before
    estimated_fix_hours = test_metadata.get("estimated_fix_hours", 1)
    # Higher impact AND lower estimated cost both increase
    # priority — a simple, reasonable ratio:
    return round(impact_score / max(estimated_fix_hours, 0.5), 2)

Why this GENUINELY matters for real prioritization:
A test with impact score 12.0 needing 1 hour to fix (per this
  page's Test B) is arguably a BETTER use of immediate time
  than a test with impact score 15.0 needing 3 DAYS to fix —
  the IMPACT-per-EFFORT ratio, not impact alone, often better
  reflects genuine, practical prioritization for a team with
  real, limited time.

This is a genuinely valid extension worth making explicit — the page's actual formula captures impact well, but a truly complete prioritization system, matching its own title fully, would reasonably weigh estimated fix effort alongside impact, not impact in isolation.
